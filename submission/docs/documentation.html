Between Passes in two minutes

The problem. Satellites find floods, harvests and cleared forest by comparing two radar pictures of the same place. But wet soil looks brighter to radar, so after rain whole regions look “changed” when nothing happened. At 147 random NISAR places, 28× more land looked changed where NASA SMAP saw the soil get wetter or drier (5.0% vs 0.18%).
The solution. Wetter soil can only brighten radar, drier soil only darken it. Between Passes asks SMAP how the soil changed between the two passes and sorts every change: one that matches the weather could be just the rain (blue); one that goes against it cannot be the weather, so something really changed (orange).
What it delivers. A free web app with 148 places in 39 countries (41 really changed, 27 mostly weather, 80 quiet); a command, bp_check.py, that runs the filter on any place NISAR has flown; a Turkish “where to look first” list of 128 fields in Ceyhan, Adana; and three predictions locked before NASA released the data.
Why trust it. Every rule was committed to GitHub before it was run. The link between NISAR change and SMAP soil moisture held at 74 random places and again at 73 new ones (ρ = 0.58 and 0.55, p < 0.001). 37 automated tests; the app re-checks its published numbers in the browser.

1 · User guide

How to use the app

Open deniz2077.github.io/between-passes on any computer or phone. No installation, no account, no login. A Turkish page is at /tr.html.

The front page: real NISAR radar over Ceyhan, filter on. Blue: could be the rain. Orange: really changed.
1
Watch the filter work. On the front page, press Before, After the rain and Filter on. The same radar map goes from “everything changed” (white) to blue and orange.
2
Read the colours. Blue: the change matches the soil moisture, so it could be just the weather. Orange: it goes against the weather, so something on the ground really changed.
3
Explore the world. Click any of 148 dots. Each card lets you drag between the two passes, switch the filter on and off, read the soil moisture and rain, and copy a share link.
4
Look at Ceyhan. “Where to look first” lists the 128 fields that got darker while the soil got wetter, largest first, with the nearest village.
5
Check the predictions. The prediction board shows three locked predictions and scores them automatically when NASA releases the passes.
6
Go deeper. The lab (lab.html) has a guided tour, field measurement, every layer and the method; world.html has the worldwide test.
World explorer: 148 places in 39 countries. Dot colour = the filter verdict.
Ceyhan: where to look first, with the nearest village.

Run it on a new place

python scripts/bp_check.py --lat 38.50 --lon -98.50 --name "Kansas, USA"

Finds two NISAR passes over the point in NASA's catalogue, reads only the needed part of each file, asks SMAP and POWER about the weather, and writes the filtered change map and a report. Needs a free NASA Earthdata account (section 11).

5 · The weather filter: rules fixed first

The weather filter: rules fixed before computing

Written and committed on 15 Nov 2026 (~11:45 TRT), before any per-pixel filter result was computed for the worldwide sites. The site-level numbers it uses (NISAR ΔHH, SMAP ΔSM) already exist from the pre-registered test; the per-pixel classification below does not.

Why

Radar change maps are how satellites watch the ground through cloud. But wet soil looks brighter to L-band radar and dry soil darker, so rain and drying make change maps light up with change that is only weather. In the worldwide test, at sites where SMAP soil moisture changed by at least 0.05 m³/m³, a median 5.1 % of the land changed by ≥ 3 dB, against 0.18 % at sites where it changed by ≤ 0.02 m³/m³ (147 sites, original + replication).

The physics gives the filter its rule: wetting can only brighten, drying can only darken. A change that goes against the soil-moisture change cannot be explained by it.

Inputs (per site, already measured)

  • NISAR L2 GCOV HH, two passes on the same track 12 days apart, same 10 × 10 km window and validity rules as the worldwide test.
  • NASA SMAP soil-moisture change ΔSM for the same days (pre-registered rule; Ceyhan uses its labelled exploratory ±2-day value).

Rules

  1. Weather signal. ΔSM ≥ +0.05 → soil got wetter; ΔSM ≤ −0.05 → drier; otherwise steady.
  2. Changed land. After the 5 × 5 linear boxcar (as in the test), pixels with ΔHH ≥ +3 dB (brighter) or ≤ −3 dB (darker). 3 dB is the threshold used for the Ceyhan change objects.
  3. Each changed pixel is
  4. could be the weather if the soil got wetter and the pixel got brighter, or the soil got drier and the pixel got darker;
  5. beyond the weather otherwise, including every changed pixel where the soil was steady.
  6. Site verdict.
  7. Changed land < 1 % of valid pixels → Quiet.
  8. Else, soil wetter or drier and ≥ 2/3 of the changed land could be the weather → Mostly the weather.
  9. Else → Real change beyond the weather.
  10. Expected shift (context only). The pre-registered fit (+1.02 dB per +0.1 m³/m³, intercept −0.13 dB) gives the site-wide change the soil moisture alone would predict; it is shown next to the measured change, not used in the verdict.

What it is and is not

  • A screening filter: it tells you which changes the weather cannot explain, so you know where to look. It does not say what the real change was (harvest, ploughing, flooding, building, fire, wind on water…).
  • "Could be the weather" is not proof of weather. Ceyhan's brightened fields are in this group; the three locked predictions test them.
  • SMAP sees 9 km cells; a local shower inside a "steady" cell would show as "beyond the weather". Vegetation growth, freezing and wind on water also change radar.
  • Thresholds (0.05 m³/m³, 3 dB, 1 %, 2/3) are choices made here, before computing; they are not tuned.

6 · Method in detail

Method — Between Passes

0. The weather filter (the product)

Rules: research/filter/WEATHER_FILTER.md (committed before computing). Code: scripts/build_sites.py (all sites), scripts/bp_check.py (any point). 1. For each place: two NISAR L2 GCOV passes on the same track and frame (identical grid), 10 × 10 km window, the validity rules below, 5 × 5 linear boxcar, ΔHH in dB. 2. NASA SMAP L3 Enhanced soil moisture for both days (overpass nearest the NISAR local time, recommended quality, ±1 day) → soil wetter (ΔSM ≥ +0.05), drier (≤ −0.05) or steady. 3. Changed land: |ΔHH| ≥ 3 dB. A changed pixel could be the weather if it moved the way the soil moisture did (wetter → brighter, drier → darker); otherwise it is beyond the weather (all changes where the soil was steady). 4. Verdict: changed land < 1 % → quiet; soil moved and ≥ 2/3 of changed land could be weather → mostly the weather; otherwise → real change beyond the weather. 5. Result over the 147 worldwide-test sites (research/filter/results.json): median changed land 5.0 % where the soil moved (n = 45) vs 0.18 % where it was steady (n = 63), 28×; pooled, 84.5 % of changed land where the soil moved could be weather. Verdicts: 41 beyond, 26 weather, 80 quiet. Ceyhan (exploratory SMAP): mostly the weather, 88 %.

Question

Between NISAR's passes on 22 Oct and 3 Nov 2026 — an interval that included the area's first significant autumn rain — which fields around Ceyhan (Adana, Türkiye) changed in L-band HH backscatter beyond a stable urban control, and what physical explanations remain possible?

1. Data selection

  • NASA CMR (short_name=NISAR_L2_GCOV_V1, point 35.8E 37.0N) returned three VALIDATED GCOV granules. Two share track 151, descending, frame 70, CRID P05036 (22 Oct and 3 Nov 2026, 16:24 UTC) and are used. The third (29 Oct, track 079) has different viewing geometry and is not compared pixel-by-pixel.
  • Quantity: diagonal covariance terms HHHH and HVHV = radiometrically terrain-corrected γ⁰ in linear power (RTC-AP, area-based; output convention gamma0, linear power).

2. Access

scripts/nisar_remote.py wraps authenticated HTTP byte-range requests in a seekable file object so h5py reads only the HDF5 chunks intersecting the window (~70 MB of 1.9 GB per granule).

3. Comparability

  • Both granules are on the identical 20 m UTM 37N geogrid (same row/column indices and coordinates) → no resampling.
  • Joint validity: subswath mask in 1…254 on both dates, inputDataExceptionMask == 768 (HH and HV fully focused, no anomaly), γ⁰ > 0 for HH and HV on both dates.
  • 500 m buffer from any invalid pixel (L2 valid-sample masks are documented as misaligned near swath edges).

4. Measurement

  • Speckle: 5×5 (100 m) valid-weighted boxcar on linear γ⁰, then dB.
  • Change: ΔdB = 10·log₁₀(γ⁰_after / γ⁰_before).
  • Regions (objects, user polygons, control): ratio of mean linear power over member pixels (not the mean of per-pixel dB).

5. Control

Largest connected cluster that is persistently bright (smoothed HH > 0 dB on both dates): the built-up centre of Ceyhan, 1,342 px. ΔHH +0.12 dB (region); per-pixel 1/5/25/50/75/95/99 % = −1.31 / −0.61 / −0.14 / +0.08 / +0.43 / +1.03 / +2.09 dB.

6. Change objects

Hysteresis on smoothed ΔHH within the analysis area: seeds |Δ| ≥ 3 dB (power ×2), grown through same-sign pixels |Δ| ≥ 2 dB (8-connectivity), minimum 25 px (1 ha). Brightening and darkening are segmented separately. The 3 dB seed exceeds the control's 99th percentile. Sensitivity to the seed (2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5 dB) is reported in the app.

7. Physical checks

  • HH: binned by brightness, ΔHH shows no trend across range; the product's noise-equivalent backscatter is ~0.3–0.5 dB lower on 3 Nov, so additive noise cannot produce the brightening. → used.
  • HV: ΔHV rises west→east even at fixed brightness (e.g. −0.15 → +1.84 dB for −15…−13 dB pixels) while HV noise changed only ~0.3 dB, and the product reports a high HV RFI likelihood (581 / 305). → shown with caution, not interpreted.

8. Optical cross-check (NASA HLS)

  • HLS S30 v2.0 surface reflectance, tile 36SYF: 23 Oct (before, 0 % cloud) and 2 Nov (after), 08:30 UTC — a consistent same-tile pair covering ~60 % of the analysis area. (Adding tile 36SYG with a 21 Oct baseline reached 86 % but created a visible NDVI seam — excluded; sensitivity: 10.4 % vegetation loss instead of 9.0 %.)
  • Fmask bits cloud, adjacent cloud/shadow, cloud shadow and snow excluded; NDVI = (B8A − B04)/(B8A + B04); reflectance average-resampled (Fmask: mode) onto the exact NISAR 20 m grid.
  • Per object (≥ 80 % pixels clear on both dates): median ΔNDVI; class vegetation loss (≤ −0.10), greening (≥ +0.10), else little vegetation change.
  • Result: of 310 brightened objects with a clear view, 9.0 % lost vegetation and 85.5 % show little vegetation change; median ΔNDVI +0.015 vs +0.060 for fields outside objects.

9. Context

NASA POWER daily PRECTOTCORR at 35.8E 37.0N: 27.63 mm between passes, 6.46 mm on 28 Oct and 16.49 mm on 29 Oct. Timing context only.

10. Verification

  • tests/test_science.py — unit tests on synthetic fixtures (dB conversion, masks, edge buffer, boxcar, region estimator, hysteresis objects, encodings).
  • tests/test_case_integrity.py — recomputes published statistics from the shipped binary layers; SHA-256 of every layer; optical class consistency.
  • Browser self-check on every load (Method & sources): control, all 636 objects, 200 parity pixels of the speckle filter and the analysis area recomputed in JavaScript; max difference ≤ 0.005 dB.

11. Global test (pre-registered)

Full plan: research/global/PREREGISTRATION.md (commit 43f2fbb, before any extraction). Results and deviations: research/global/RESULTS.md. - Population: 889 NISAR VALIDATED HH repeat pairs (same track/frame/CRID) in CMR, 21 Oct–11 Nov 2026; |lat| ≤ 60° → 763; simple random sample of 200 (seed 2026). - NISAR metric: 10 × 10 km window at the frame centre, joint validity as above, ΔHH_site = 10·log₁₀(mean γ⁰_B / mean γ⁰_A). - Independent measurement: NASA SMAP L3 Enhanced 9 km soil moisture (SPL3SMP_E), overpass nearest the NISAR local solar time, recommended quality only, ±1 day. - Test: Spearman ρ, two-sided permutation p (10,000), Theil–Sen slope with bootstrap CI. - Result: n = 74 retained (125 lacked approved SMAP values, 1 invalid window); ρ = 0.577, p = 0.0001, +1.02 dB per +0.1 m³/m³ (95 % CI 0.69–1.42). - Ceyhan: the pre-registered SMAP rule finds no approved value near 22 Oct; an exploratory ±2-day window gives 0.161 → 0.356 m³/m³. - Replication (pre-registered, research/global/REPLICATION_PREREGISTRATION.md, commit 7e1ffcf): 200 new pairs (seed 2027, disjoint from the original sample), same rules → n = 73, ρ = 0.546, p = 0.0001, +0.78 dB per +0.1 m³/m³ (95 % CI 0.45–1.11). Replication criterion met. Pooled (descriptive): n = 147, ρ = 0.572.

8 · Prediction 1: the next pass

A falsifiable prediction for NISAR's next pass over Ceyhan

Locked and published before the satellite flies over (14 Nov 2026, evening TRT; git commit and push timestamp on GitHub). Next pass: NISAR track 151 descending, frame 70, cycle 035, expected 15 Nov 2026 ~16:24 UTC. Machine-readable lock: prediction.json. Scorer: scripts/predict_next_pass.py score <granule>.

Why this is a real test

Between 22 Oct and 3 Nov, 508 Ceyhan parcels brightened (pooled +4.14 dB). Two explanations remain: - Wet soil (rain on 28–29 Oct). NASA SMAP says Ceyhan's soil has since dried: 0.36 m³/m³ on 3 Nov → 0.23 (5 Nov) → 0.13 (10 Nov) → 0.10 (11 Nov). If wetting caused the brightening, these parcels will dim back. - Rougher soil (ploughing). Roughness does not dry out. If ploughing caused it, they will stay bright.

The next pass separates them. We commit to the criteria now.

Criteria (fixed)

  • P1 — aggregate: pooled over all 508 brightened parcels, ΔHH(3 Nov → 15 Nov) ≤ −2.07 dB (at least half the brightening reverses).
  • P2 — typical parcel: median recovery R ≥ 0.5, where R = −ΔHH(B→C) / ΔHH(A→B) (1 = back to 22 Oct, 0 = stayed bright).
  • P3 — sanity: Ceyhan's urban control moves by ≤ 0.5 dB; otherwise the pass is not comparable.
  • Verdict: P1 and P2 met → wetting dominant, confirmed. Median R < 0.25 → persistent: roughness/ploughing dominant. Otherwise mixed. Every parcel also gets a label: dried back (R ≥ 0.5) / partial / stayed bright (R < 0.25).

When the test is void

  • The cycle-035 granule is missing, not VALIDATED, or not on the identical grid.
  • It rained again: SMAP at Ceyhan (approved retrieval 14–16 Nov) ≥ 0.25 m³/m³, or, without SMAP, NASA POWER ≥ 10 mm over 12–15 Nov.

Honest notes

  • NISAR VALIDATED data have appeared about 3–6 days after acquisition, so the result is expected after the NASA submission deadline. The prediction is locked now; the score will be published in the app when the data exist.
  • Rain fell on 4–6 Nov (13.6 mm, NASA POWER), after our second pass; SMAP shows the soil dried again by 10–11 Nov.

9 · Prediction 2: two other orbits

Prediction 2: the same fields, seen from two other orbits

Locked and published on 15 Nov 2026 (03:20 TRT, commit a068d8f), before NASA released the data it predicts. The git commit on GitHub is the timestamp. Machine-readable lock: prediction2.json. Scorer: scripts/predict_two_orbits.py.

In plain words

Between 22 October and 3 November, 508 fields near Ceyhan lit up on radar. My explanation is that the soil got wet in the first big autumn rain. The other possible explanation is ploughing, which also makes a field look brighter.

NISAR does not only fly over Ceyhan on the orbit I studied. It also passes on two other orbits, one at dusk and one at dawn. Both flew over on 29 October, the rainy day, and again on 10 November, when the soil had dried (NASA's SMAP satellite: 0.29 m³/m³ on 28 Oct, 0.13 on 10 Nov; no rain 7–11 Nov in NASA POWER).

My prediction: on both of these orbits, the 508 fields will get darker from 29 Oct to 10 Nov, by more than the farmland around them. Wet soil dries out; ploughed soil stays rough. If they stay bright, my explanation is wrong.

Each orbit is a separate, independent check. It sees the ground from a different angle and at a different time of day, and it never took part in choosing the fields.

The satellite had already flown on 10 November, but NASA had not released those data when I locked this. I checked NASA's catalogue just before locking: no 10 November pass over Ceyhan was listed for either orbit.

Criteria (fixed)

For each orbit separately, comparing 29 Oct with 10 Nov on that orbit's own grid: - Q1: the brightened fields, pooled together, darken by at least 1.0 dB more than the surrounding farmland. - Q2: at least two thirds of the brightened fields darken more than the surrounding farmland. - Q3 (sanity): Ceyhan's town centre changes by no more than 0.5 dB; otherwise the orbit is not comparable, and the result is inconclusive. - Verdict: Q1 and Q2 met → confirmed (wet soil). The fields stay as bright as their surroundings or get brighter → refuted (ploughing). Anything else → mixed.

Exact definitions are in prediction2.json (criteria).

How the fields are matched

The field outlines come from the original track-151 analysis. They are carried onto each orbit's own grid by geographic position, using the nearest pixel. Radar values are never resampled. Before locking, I checked the alignment: the 29 Oct images of both orbits match the 3 Nov track-151 image best at zero shift (to within 20 m). 494 of the 508 fields fall inside the track 079 window; all 508 fall inside track 071.

When the test is void

  • The 10 Nov granule for an orbit is missing, not VALIDATED, or not on the identical grid.
  • Rain before the pass (NASA POWER ≥ 10 mm over 7–10 Nov). Already known at lock: 0 mm on each day, so this condition is met.

Honest notes

  • Interference flag. NASA's metadata gives both 29 Oct passes a high radio-interference likelihood for the whole frame: about 56,000 for track 071 and about 40 billion for track 079, against about 6 for the track-151 pair. Over the Ceyhan window, the images show no visible interference stripes, and the data-quality mask is clean (every pixel code 768). If interference affected 29 Oct, the town-centre check (Q3) is meant to catch it.
  • Not a repeat of the same measurement. These orbits see the town and fields from other angles. That is why each orbit is only compared with itself, and the fields with the farmland around them.
  • Already visible on 29 Oct. Compared with their surroundings, the fields that brightened were already partly brighter on 29 Oct. This is not part of the test, but it fits rain on 28–29 Oct.

10 · Limitations: what I don't claim

Limitations

  1. Two radar dates. One pair cannot distinguish lasting change (tillage) from transient change (wet soil drying out).
  2. Cause is not measured. HH backscatter responds to soil moisture, roughness, vegetation structure and water. Interpretations are labelled consistent / possible / not indicated, never confirmed.
  3. Spatial correlation. Pixels after multilooking and filtering are not independent; I compare against a control instead of reporting independent-pixel confidence intervals.
  4. Urban control. The control demonstrates instrument/processing stability, not how an unchanged agricultural field behaves.
  5. HV unresolved. West–east ΔHV gradient at fixed brightness and high RFI likelihood; HV is not interpreted.
  6. Thermal noise not subtracted in the product (noiseCorrectionApplied = False); very dark pixels carry a larger noise share.
  7. Swath edge. West of the window is outside the swath; a 500 m buffer is applied.
  8. Thresholds are choices. 3 dB seed / 2 dB grow / 1 ha; sensitivity 2–5 dB shown. The brightened ÷ darkened area ratio stays between 7× and 11×.
  9. Optical cross-check. HLS dates differ from radar by up to ~1.5 days; only ~60 % of the analysis area has a consistent cloud-free same-tile pair (the northern tile would need a different baseline date and showed a seam, so it is excluded). NDVI cannot separate wet from ploughed bare soil.
  10. Rainfall is a NASA POWER gridded estimate (~0.5°), not a gauge.
  11. Single case study. Not an operational monitoring or alert system.

Global test

  • Site-scale (10 km windows), one 12-day interval per site, one random sample; not field-scale.
  • 63 % of drawn sites lacked a quality-approved SMAP retrieval, so retained sites lean toward land covers where SMAP retrieves well.
  • SMAP's 9 km cell and the NISAR window are not identical footprints; SMAP measures the top ~5 cm.
  • Ceyhan's SMAP comparison is exploratory (±2-day window, not pre-registered), and its wetting (+0.195 m³/m³) is larger than at any sampled site, so the global fit is extrapolated there.
  • SMAP shows that Ceyhan's soil got wetter; it cannot say which individual fields, so ploughing remains possible for any single parcel.

The predictions

  • The three checks test the same 508 fields, so they are not three independent samples of fields; they are independent observations (different passes, and for Prediction 2, different viewing angles and times of day).
  • Prediction 2 carries the track-151 field outlines onto the other orbits' grids by position (nearest 20 m pixel). Alignment was checked before locking (best match at zero shift), but edges of small fields can shift by a pixel.
  • NASA's frame-level radio-interference flag is high for both 29 Oct passes. The Ceyhan window shows no visible interference and a clean quality mask; the town-centre check would flag a problem.
  • A confirmed prediction supports wet soil as the main cause overall; it still cannot prove the cause for any single field.

The weather filter

  • It is a screening filter: it says which changes the weather cannot explain, not what the real change was (harvest, ploughing, flooding, building, fire, wind on water…).
  • "Could be the weather" is not proof of weather. Ceyhan's brightened fields fall in this group; the predictions test them.
  • SMAP measures 9 km cells and the top ~5 cm of soil. A local shower inside a "steady" cell shows up as "beyond the weather".
  • Vegetation growth, freezing and wind over water also change radar; the filter does not model them.
  • Site cards use one 10 km window per place, picked at random; many places are not farmland.
  • Thresholds (0.05 m³/m³, 3 dB, 1 %, 2/3) are choices fixed before computing, not tuned.
  • The demonstration places run with bp_check.py were chosen by name (farming regions), not at random, and are not part of any statistical test.
